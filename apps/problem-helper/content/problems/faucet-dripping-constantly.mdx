# Faucet Dripping Constantly: Fix Leaky Faucet Step-by-Step

A constantly dripping faucet is more than annoying—it wastes significant water and money. A faucet dripping once per second wastes over 3,000 gallons annually, adding $30-60 to water bills. Most dripping faucets result from worn internal components (washers, O-rings, cartridges) that are surprisingly easy to replace. This guide covers all common faucet types and provides step-by-step repair instructions for DIY fixes that typically cost under $20 and take 30-60 minutes.

## Identifying Your Faucet Type

Different faucet designs require different repair approaches:

**Compression faucets:** Separate hot and cold handles that turn multiple rotations. Oldest design, uses rubber washers that wear out. Most common in older homes.

**Ball faucets:** Single handle that moves in all directions. Contains ball-shaped mechanism with springs and seals. Common in kitchens.

**Cartridge faucets:** Single or double handle with smooth, consistent movement. Internal cartridge controls water flow. Very common in modern homes.

**Ceramic disk faucets:** Single lever over wide base. Most durable design with ceramic disks that rarely wear out. Higher-end faucets.

Identifying your type determines which parts you need and repair steps to follow.

## Tools and Materials Needed

**Basic tools:**
- Adjustable wrench or pliers
- Screwdrivers (Phillips and flathead)
- Allen wrench set (for handle removal)
- Flashlight
- Towels or rags

**Materials (varies by faucet type):**
- Replacement washers, O-rings, or cartridge
- Plumber's grease
- Vinegar (for cleaning mineral deposits)
- Replacement parts specific to your faucet brand

**Pro tip:** Take photos before disassembly and bring old parts to hardware store for exact matching.

## General Preparation Steps

Before starting any faucet repair:

**1. Turn off water supply:**
- Locate shutoff valves under sink (turn clockwise)
- If no shutoff valves, turn off main water supply
- Turn on faucet to release remaining pressure
- Verify water is off

**2. Plug the drain:**
- Use sink stopper or rag to block drain
- Prevents small screws and parts from falling down drain

**3. Gather parts:**
- Know your faucet brand and model if possible
- Have replacement parts ready before disassembly
- Common brands: Delta, Moen, Kohler, American Standard, Pfister

**4. Protect sink:**
- Place towel in sink to prevent scratches
- Protects finish from dropped tools

## Fixing Compression Faucets

Most common cause: worn washer at end of stem.

**Steps:**

1. **Remove handle:**
   - Pry off decorative cap on handle top
   - Unscrew handle screw (may be Phillips or flathead)
   - Lift off handle

2. **Remove packing nut:**
   - Use adjustable wrench to unscrew packing nut
   - Turn counterclockwise
   - Lift out stem assembly

3. **Inspect and replace washer:**
   - At bottom of stem, you'll see rubber washer held by brass screw
   - Remove screw and old washer
   - Clean away any sediment
   - Install new washer (exact same size)
   - Replace brass screw

4. **Check O-ring:**
   - Inspect O-ring on stem body
   - If worn or damaged, carefully remove and replace
   - Coat new O-ring with plumber's grease

5. **Reassemble:**
   - Insert stem back into faucet body
   - Tighten packing nut snugly (don't overtighten)
   - Replace handle and screw
   - Replace decorative cap

6. **Test:**
   - Turn water supply back on slowly
   - Check for leaks
   - Operate handle to verify proper function

**Cost:** $2-5 for washer assortment pack

## Fixing Ball Faucets

More complex mechanism with multiple small parts.

**Steps:**

1. **Remove handle:**
   - Loosen setscrew on handle side (usually Allen wrench)
   - Lift off handle

2. **Remove cap and collar:**
   - Unscrew cap using adjustable pliers
   - Lift off cam and cam washer

3. **Remove ball:**
   - Lift out ball assembly
   - Inspect for wear or corrosion

4. **Replace springs and seals:**
   - Use needle-nose pliers to remove inlet seals and springs
   - Remove both sets (hot and cold)
   - Install new springs first, then new seals over them
   - Press firmly into place

5. **Install new ball (if worn):**
   - Align slot in ball with pin in faucet body
   - Ensure proper orientation

6. **Replace cam and collar:**
   - Install new cam washer
   - Replace cap and tighten firmly

7. **Reassemble handle:**
   - Replace handle and tighten setscrew
   - Ensure handle moves smoothly in all directions

**Better option:** Many hardware stores sell complete ball faucet repair kits ($15-25) containing all commonly worn parts. Usually easier and more cost-effective than individual parts.

## Fixing Cartridge Faucets

Cartridge replacement is straightforward once you access it.

**Steps:**

1. **Remove handle:**
   - Pry off cap if present
   - Remove screw securing handle
   - Lift off handle

2. **Remove retaining clip or nut:**
   - Some cartridges have U-shaped retaining clip—pull out with pliers
   - Others have threaded retaining nut—unscrew

3. **Remove cartridge:**
   - Grip cartridge stem with pliers
   - Pull straight up while slightly twisting
   - May require significant force if old

4. **Install new cartridge:**
   - Ensure you have exact replacement for your brand
   - Check cartridge orientation (usually has flat side or tabs)
   - Insert fully into body
   - Install retaining clip or nut

5. **Reassemble:**
   - Replace handle and secure with screw
   - Replace decorative cap

6. **Test:**
   - Turn on water supply
   - Check for leaks
   - Verify hot/cold orientation is correct

**If hot/cold is reversed:** Remove cartridge, rotate 180°, and reinstall.

**Cost:** Cartridges vary by brand, typically $15-40.

## Fixing Ceramic Disk Faucets

Most reliable design; drips usually indicate debris or worn seals rather than disk failure.

**Steps:**

1. **Remove handle:**
   - Loosen setscrew
   - Lift off handle

2. **Remove disk cylinder:**
   - Unscrew cap
   - Lift out ceramic disk cylinder

3. **Clean seals and cylinder:**
   - Remove inlet seals from bottom of cylinder
   - Clean all parts with vinegar to remove mineral deposits
   - Inspect seals for nicks or wear

4. **Replace seals if worn:**
   - Install new inlet seals (available at hardware stores)
   - Ensure proper seating

5. **Clean cylinder body:**
   - Remove any sediment from faucet body
   - Flush with water if possible

6. **Reassemble:**
   - Replace cylinder in body
   - Screw on cap
   - Replace handle and setscrew

7. **Turn on water SLOWLY:**
   - Sudden water pressure can crack ceramic disks
   - Open supply valves gradually

**Note:** Ceramic disks rarely fail. If problem persists after cleaning and seal replacement, disk cylinder replacement may be needed ($50-100+ depending on brand).

## Special Cases

### Faucet Drips from Base (Not Spout)

Indicates O-ring failure around faucet body.

**Fix:**
- Remove entire faucet from sink
- Locate O-rings around base
- Replace with exact size O-rings
- Apply plumber's grease before reassembly

### Faucet Drips Only When Turned Off Certain Way

Indicates worn stem or cartridge needing replacement rather than just washer/seal.

### Two-Handle Cartridge Faucet Drips

Follow cartridge replacement procedure for the dripping side only (hot or cold).

## When Parts Are Unavailable

For older or discontinued faucet models:

**Options:**
- Check manufacturer website for parts—many stock old parts
- Call manufacturer customer service with model number
- Search online for third-party or OEM replacement parts
- Consider faucet replacement if parts unavailable or too expensive

**Faucet replacement vs. repair decision:**
- If repair parts cost more than $50, consider replacement
- If faucet is over 15 years old, replacement may offer better value
- New faucets cost $40-200 for decent quality

## Preventing Future Drips

**Water quality matters:**
- Hard water accelerates wear on seals and washers
- Consider water softener if you have very hard water
- Flush sediment from water heater annually

**Proper use:**
- Don't overtighten handles when turning off
- Use gentle, consistent pressure
- Address small leaks immediately before they worsen

**Maintenance:**
- Replace washers/seals every 3-5 years proactively in high-use faucets
- Clean aerators regularly to prevent sediment buildup
- Check under-sink shutoff valves annually—operate them to prevent seizing

## Troubleshooting Persistent Drips

If drip continues after repair:

**Check these:**
- Washer/seal size—must be exact fit
- Proper orientation of cartridge or disk
- Valve seat condition (see below)
- Water pressure (excessive pressure can cause drips)

### Damaged Valve Seat

In compression faucets, the valve seat (brass ring inside faucet body) can corrode or pit, preventing new washer from sealing properly.

**Diagnosis:** After installing new washer, faucet still drips.

**Solutions:**
1. **Surface it:** Use valve seat wrench (special tool) to smooth surface
2. **Replace it:** Some seats are removable—unscrew with seat wrench and replace
3. **Call plumber:** If seat is corroded and non-removable, may require faucet replacement

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: How long should a faucet repair last?**

Quality replacement parts should last 3-5 years for high-use faucets, 5-10 years for light use. Compression faucet washers wear fastest.

**Q: Why does my faucet drip only when hot water is on?**

Hot water wears rubber parts faster. Replace hot water side components first.

**Q: Can I use any washer or O-ring?**

No—size must match exactly. Take old part to hardware store for matching. Using wrong size causes immediate or near-term failure.

**Q: My faucet still drips after replacing everything. What now?**

Possible valve seat damage, incorrect part installation, or damaged faucet body. May need professional assessment or faucet replacement.

**Q: How much water does a dripping faucet waste?**

One drip per second = 3,000 gallons/year = about $35-60 in water/sewer costs. Faster drips waste proportionally more.

**Q: Is it normal for a brand new faucet to drip?**

Slight dripping for first day can be normal as parts settle. If continues beyond 24 hours, return as defective or check installation.

## Conclusion

Most dripping faucets are easily fixed with basic tools and inexpensive parts. Compression faucets need washer replacement, ball faucets need springs and seals, cartridge faucets need cartridge replacement, and ceramic disk faucets usually just need cleaning.

The key is correctly identifying your faucet type, obtaining the right replacement parts, and following systematic disassembly and reassembly. Taking photos during disassembly prevents confusion during reassembly.

Don't ignore dripping faucets—they waste thousands of gallons of water annually and worsen over time. A 30-minute DIY repair costing under $20 eliminates the annoying drip, saves water, and reduces utility bills. For faucets where parts are unavailable or repairs exceed $50, consider upgrading to a new, more water-efficient model.
